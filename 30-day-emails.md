EMAIL 3 — Day 4
Subject: What most people get wrong about AI prompts
Preview: It's not about magic words.

Hi [First Name],

There's a whole industry built around "prompt engineering" — special phrases, secret formulas, optimized structures that supposedly unlock AI's true power.

Some of it is genuinely useful. Most of it is overcomplicated.

Here's what I've found after years of teaching AI to professionals: the people who get the best results from AI aren't the ones who memorize the cleverest prompts. They're the ones who know how to give context.

Think about how you'd brief a smart new employee. You wouldn't just say "write a report." You'd say: here's who this is for, here's what they already know, here's what decision they're trying to make, here's the tone we use, and here's what I've already tried.

That's a prompt.

Context has five layers:
1. Role — who is the AI being in this task? (e.g., "Act as a business strategist...")
2. Task — what specifically are you asking for?
3. Audience — who will read or use the output?
4. Format — how do you want it structured?
5. Constraints — what should it avoid or stay within?

You don't need all five every time. But the more context you give, the less back-and-forth you need.

I teach this framework in my AI Content Creation course, and it's the single thing that gets students from "the AI output is garbage" to "I actually used that." Not a magic phrase. Just better context.

Try it today with one task you've been putting off.

EMAIL 7 — Day 15
Subject: The biggest AI mistake I see business owners make
Preview: It costs time and money.

Hi [First Name],

After working with 15+ business clients on AI implementation, I've seen every kind of mistake. But one comes up more than any other.

Automating a broken process.

A business owner reads about AI automation. Gets excited. Spends two weeks (and sometimes serious money) building an automated workflow for something. Then discovers the output is consistently wrong, the team doesn't trust it, and now they've automated their chaos instead of fixing it.

AI is a multiplier. It makes good processes faster and bad processes more chaotic. Before you automate anything, you need to understand the process well enough to describe it clearly to a very literal-minded assistant who has no context, no intuition, and no ability to fill in the gaps you left.

If you can't write down the exact steps of a process, you're not ready to automate it.

I learned this the hard way when I was scaling one of my early AI implementations. I had a content workflow that "kind of" worked — people generally knew what to do, but the steps lived in people's heads. When I tried to translate that into an AI-assisted workflow, it fell apart immediately, because the ambiguity that humans could navigate through judgment became errors when AI tried to navigate it through rules.

We spent three weeks fixing the workflow documentation first. Then the AI implementation took four days.

The fix: before you automate, document. Write down every step. Every decision point. Every edge case. Then — and only then — look at where AI can speed things up.

EMAIL 10 — Day 24
Subject: The ROI question every business owner should ask
Preview: Before buying any AI tool.

Hi [First Name],

Every week I hear from a business owner who's spent money on an AI tool that isn't delivering value. Sometimes significant money.

The common thread: they bought before they answered the ROI question.

The ROI question isn't "is this tool impressive?" AI tools are almost all impressive in demos. The ROI question is: "What specific outcome in my business will improve if I use this tool consistently for 90 days, and how will I measure it?"

If you can't answer that in one sentence, don't buy the tool yet.

Before any AI purchase, answer three questions:

1. What is the current cost of the problem? (Time per week × hourly value, or lost revenue, or error rate × cost per error)
2. What is the realistic improvement? (Not the demo. The realistic, conservative improvement after the learning curve.)
3. What is the payback period? (If the tool costs $50/month and saves 4 hours/month at $30/hour, payback is immediate. If it costs $500/month and you're not sure what it replaces, that's a problem.)

This isn't about being cheap. It's about being intentional. The best AI implementations aren't the ones with the biggest budgets — they're the ones with the clearest problem definitions.

Know what you're solving before you open your wallet.

Here's one research finding that's stuck with me — and that I think about constantly in my AI consulting work.

When new technology enters an organization, the people who resist it aren't usually resistant to change in general. They're resistant to uncertainty about their own competence.

People are less afraid of the technology than they are afraid of looking incompetent in front of colleagues while learning it.

This has huge implications for how you introduce AI to your team. If you announce "we're implementing AI now" and leave people to figure it out themselves, you're asking them to learn it publicly, in front of colleagues, with no safety net.

The fastest way to slow down AI adoption is to make people afraid of looking stupid. The fastest way to accelerate it is to make looking stupid safe.

Budget at least as much time for the human side as you do for the technical side. Often more.

EMAIL 27 — Day 75
Subject: The three types of AI value in any business
Preview: A simple framework that clarifies everything.

Hi [First Name],

After years of AI consulting, I've landed on a simple way to categorize AI value. Three types. Every legitimate AI use case fits into one.

Type 1: Speed value. AI does something you already do, faster. Writing, research, analysis, documentation — same quality or better, in a fraction of the time. This is the most immediate, most accessible form of AI value, available to almost every business right now.

Type 2: Scale value. AI lets you do something you couldn't afford to do before because it required too many people or too much time. Personalized communication at scale. 24/7 customer engagement. Consistent quality across a large team. This is where AI starts to change business models.

Type 3: Capability value. AI gives you a capability you simply didn't have before. Analysis of data sets too large for humans to process. Pattern recognition across thousands of customer interactions. This is rarer to implement well, but it's where competitive advantage lives.

Most businesses start at Type 1 and stay there. That's fine — the ROI is real.

The businesses building something durable are moving toward Type 2 and, where it fits, Type 3.

What type of AI value is your business capturing? And what type are you missing?
